# How to use this primer

This paper is the sibling of the $\Phi_2$ paper. It attacks the same kind of question (where a Goldbach-type Dirichlet series stops continuing) but proves its boundary implications from a zero hypothesis that RH implies and that allows exceptions to RH, and it treats five series, four of which are *not* single-valued. That last fact is what makes the paper harder than the $\Phi_2$ paper, and it is where this primer spends most of its words. The conclusions of the boundary theorems and the full-catalogue theorem hold unconditionally by the written deduction in Corollary 1.4. If the certified Lean proof of RH were withdrawn, Theorems 1.1–1.3 would stand as stated under $(\mathrm H_\infty)$, and Theorem 10.1 would stand as stated under RH. Only the unconditional statements in the paper, Corollary 1.4 and the paragraph after it, depend on that proof.

You already studied the $\Phi_2$ primer. A great deal of the background here is identical: Dirichlet series and half-planes of convergence, the von Mangoldt function, the zeta function and its zeros, holomorphic and meromorphic functions, the identity theorem, residues, the Gamma function, Mellin transforms, Cauchy's theorem and contour shifts, the dominant-term (head–tail) principle. For each of those I give a one-line reminder and a pointer to the matching section of the $\Phi_2$ primer, and teach in full only what is new to this paper or used differently here.

The new material, taught from scratch below, is in five clusters:

1. **The prime zeta function.** $P(z)=\sum_p p^{-z}$ and its derivative $Q=-P'$, the candidate set $\Sigma$, the Möbius expansion, and the coefficient $c(a)$. The paper is built on $P$ and $Q$, not on $\zeta'/\zeta$ directly as the $\Phi_2$ paper was.
2. **The hypothesis.** $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$, the quantities $B$ and $B_\infty$, why RH implies $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$, and the three further classical inputs (Z4), (Z5), (Z6).
3. **Multivaluedness.** Logarithmic singularities, branches, germs, analytic continuation along paths, monodromy, the jump of $\operatorname{Log}$ across a cut, Hadamard and Borel's cut-and-contour construction, and the pinch (Picard–Lefschetz) singularity. This is the central difficulty of the paper and the part of complex analysis that has faded most for you, so it gets the most room.
4. **The arithmetic.** Density of zeros on the critical line (Hardy–Littlewood, with the distinctness subtlety), primes in ratio intervals, and how the counting replaces the additive-relations hypothesis of earlier work.
5. **Two more kernels.** The series $G_2$ and $G_{\log}$ of Section 9: a kernel with halved arguments, and a pinch in which a logarithm meets a pole.

Each topic has the same shape as in the $\Phi_2$ primer:

- **Statement.** The fact, with its conditions.
- **Worked example.** Small enough to check by hand.
- **Where the paper uses it.** Section, lemma and theorem numbers.

The theorem and lemma numbers follow the current LaTeX auxiliary file; the paper numbers theorems within each section (Theorem 1.1, Lemma 2.1, and so on), with lemmas, propositions, corollaries and remarks sharing one counter per section. Equations are numbered (1) to (13) through the paper, and displays without a number are cited by where they stand; the stable item tags, (Z1)–(Z6) and (K1)–(K3), are quoted directly from the source and need no check.

Read the primer once through, then read the paper with it open. The last four sections give a one-page map of the proof, a reading order, the questions a referee might ask, and a self-test with answers. The goal is that you can answer a referee's questions about the paper, not reproduce it.

Two conventions, as in the $\Phi_2$ primer. "Analytic" and "holomorphic" mean the same thing (complex-differentiable on an open set, hence a convergent power series near each point). The paper's $\int_{(c)}f\,dz$ means $\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}f(z)\,dz$, the integral up the vertical line $\operatorname{Re}z=c$, without the factor $1/2\pi i$, which the paper writes out wherever it occurs.

# What the paper proves, in one page

**The objects.** Summing over *ordered* pairs of primes $p,q$, the paper studies five Dirichlet series:
$$G(s)=\sum_{p,q}\frac1{(p+q)^s},\qquad T(s)=\sum_{p,q}\frac{\log p\,\log q}{(p+q)^s},\qquad S(s)=\sum_{p,q}\frac{p^{1-s}q^{2-s}}{(p^2+q^2)^2},$$
$$G_2(s)=\sum_{p,q}\frac1{(p^2+q^2)^s},\qquad G_{\log}(s)=\sum_{p,q}\frac{\log q}{(p+q)^s}.$$
$G$ and $T$ count representations of an integer as a sum of two primes (without and with logarithmic weights): they are generating series of the Goldbach problem. $G$, $T$ and $G_{\log}$ converge absolutely for $\operatorname{Re}s>2$, $G_2$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, and $S$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>\tfrac12$. $S$ is included as a stress test of the method: its kernel is a different shape, decays at half the rate, and the paper's contour construction for it (the sheets of Topic 13) is set up only on a strip. $G_2$ and $G_{\log}$ probe the two places where the method could depend on the particular series: $G_2$ has a third kernel and its boundary on a different line, and $G_{\log}$ carries the logarithmic weight on one prime only, so it lies between $G$ and $T$.

**The hypothesis.** The boundary theorems use the following property of the zeros
$$(\mathrm{H}_\infty)\qquad\text{for every }\delta>0,\text{ only finitely many zeros }\rho\text{ of }\zeta\text{ have }\operatorname{Re}\rho>\tfrac12+\delta.$$
RH implies $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$, and so does "finitely many zeros lie off the critical line", so $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ allows exceptions to RH. By Backlund's criterion it implies the Lindelöf hypothesis. Section 1.2 displays $\mathrm{RH}\Rightarrow(\mathrm{H}_\infty)\Rightarrow\mathrm{LH}$ and $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)\Rightarrow B<1$. Corollary 1.4 supplies RH using Alpöge's certified Lean proof, so the conclusions of the three boundary theorems and the full-catalogue statement of Theorem 10.1 hold unconditionally. If that proof were withdrawn, Theorems 1.1–1.3 would still stand under $(\mathrm H_\infty)$ and Theorem 10.1 under RH.

The zero hypothesis is used at two strengths. The arithmetic in Section 3 and the corresponding argument for $G_2$ in Section 9.1 need only $B<1$, where $B$ is the supremum of the real parts of the non-trivial zeros. The analytic construction starts from the classical inputs. Its contour bounds depend on the locations of the zeros, and its domain is governed by $B_\infty$, the limiting supremum at large absolute height. In the collision variable $v$ the left edge is $\Re v=2B_\infty$. This gives a half-plane for four series and a strip for $S$. The condition $(\mathrm H_\infty)$ sets $2B_\infty=1$ and thereby places that edge on the boundary line in each series' own variable. The paper leaves open what continuation $B<1$ alone would give up to those lines.

**What is new.** Landau and Walfisz proved in 1920 that $\operatorname{Re}z=0$ is a natural boundary of the prime zeta function $P(z)=\sum_p p^{-z}$. That says nothing directly about the five series. Here are the three boundary implications stated under $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$. Corollary 1.4 supplies that hypothesis.

- (Theorem 1.1) $T$ has a single-valued meromorphic continuation to $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, and every disc centred on the line $\operatorname{Re}s=1$ contains infinitely many of its poles. So $\operatorname{Re}s=1$ is a natural boundary of $T$ in the ordinary meromorphic sense.
- (Theorem 1.2) $\operatorname{Re}s=1$ is a natural boundary of $G$, and $\operatorname{Re}s=0$ of $S$, *on every branch*: no admissible germ of $G$ or $S$ extends meromorphically across any point of the line.
- (Theorem 1.3) The same on every branch for the two further series: $\operatorname{Re}s=\tfrac12$ is a natural boundary of $G_2$ and $\operatorname{Re}s=1$ of $G_{\log}$. The theorem also says that each of the two continues along every path that stays in its half-plane and avoids its catalogue, and that admissible germs exist at exactly the points of the half-plane off the catalogue.

There are two path statements. The sheet construction describes a germ reached along a path avoiding the candidate points in its domain. For $S$, Theorem 7.4(b) also permits excursions out of the strip to the right, provided continuation exists and the path ends back in the strip. Under $(\mathrm H_\infty)$ the boundary argument handles arbitrary paths of analytic continuation. Corollary 8.3 for $G,S$ and Theorem 1.3(c) for $G_2,G_{\log}$ give admissibility when the endpoint lies off the catalogue. At a catalogue endpoint, the boundary obstruction instead uses an admissible germ at a nearby point in the same holomorphic representative.

The remark at the end of the proof of Theorem 1.3(c) covers the remaining endpoints. If continuation exists and ends at a catalogue point, the final holomorphic representative still has a disc on which all nearby germs off the catalogue are admissible. A meromorphic extension of the terminal germ to a boundary-centred disc would agree near the endpoint and hence at some such nearby point inside both discs. That would extend an admissible germ against Theorem 1.3(b). The remark is separate from the numbered theorem. PrimePairNB checks it as `HInf.no_extension_any_path_Sq` and `HInf.no_extension_any_path_mix`, each from `Hinf` and (Z1)–(Z6).

The paper says it knows of no earlier proof of Theorems 1.1, 1.2 or 1.3 under this hypothesis, nor of a boundary for $G$, $S$, $G_2$ or $G_{\log}$ on every branch.

**The difficulty, and the two jobs.** Each series is built from $P$ by a Mellin–Barnes integral, so inside the half-plane where it is continued its singularities can sit, in the variable $v$ (here $v=s$ for $G$, $T$ and $G_{\log}$; $v=2s+1$ for $S$, so $s=(a+b-1)/2$; and $v=2s$ for $G_2$, so $s=(a+b)/2$), only at sums $a+b$ of singular points of $P$. These sums are the candidate points. Under RH a candidate point with $\operatorname{Re}v>1$ has a single contributing unordered pair: every point of $\Sigma$ other than $1$ then has real part at most $\tfrac12$, so a pair whose sum has real part above $1$ must contain $1$ (as ordered pairs, $(1,u)$ and $(u,1)$, or the one pair $(1,1)$ at $v=2$). Without RH, zeros to the right of the critical line let several pairs sum to one point, and their contributions might cancel. The proof has two separate jobs that meet in one quantity, the coefficient $X_x$ of the logarithm at a candidate point $x$:

1. *The arithmetic* (Section 3). Show that at a dense set of candidate points the sum over all colliding pairs is nonzero. This is $X_x'(x)\ne0$.
2. *The analysis* (Sections 5–8). Turn a nonzero coefficient into an obstruction to continuation, and make the obstruction hold on *every* branch. For $T$, which is single-valued, the argument is "poles accumulate" (Section 4). For $G$ and $S$, multivalued, it needs a single-valued object (a *sheet*), a local model near each candidate point, and a proof that the coefficient is the same on all branches.

Section 9 then changes one layer for each new series and leaves the rest as it stands. For $G_2$ the kernel changes, so the arithmetic is run again for a new sum. For $G_{\log}$ one factor of the integrand is $Q$ where $G$ has $P$, so a logarithm meets a pole, the local calculation becomes a residue, and the arithmetic of $G$ and $T$ is reused unchanged. Table 1 of the paper (the strategy map, Section 1.4) lists, for each series, what meets at a pinch, the quantity that must be non-zero, and where each step is proved.

The calculation in Section 6 isolates each collision with a short contour arc. The outer contour passes through the collision points at $v=x$ and separates the other left and right points. After the small pieces around collisions are removed, the rest has enough clearance to stay fixed as $v$ changes near $x$. Its integral is holomorphic there. Each short arc accounts for one ordered pair and yields its own logarithmic term. This explains why the coefficient is a finite sum of pair integrals for two logarithms and a finite sum of kernel values when a logarithm meets a pole.

**What it rests on.** $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$, and the classical facts (Z1)–(Z6) about the zeros of $\zeta$ and the Gamma function, cited to Titchmarsh, Hardy and Littlewood, and the DLMF. Theorems 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 8.2 and 10.1 and Corollary 8.3 are checked in Lean 4 as implications: for the $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ results, Lean takes that hypothesis and the applicable classical inputs among (Z1)–(Z6) and verifies they imply the stated results (Section 11, Appendix A). Theorem 10.1 is checked from RH alone.

Corollary 1.4 is a written deduction. Alpöge's theorem supplies RH for Theorem 10.1. Under RH every non-trivial zero has real part $\tfrac12$, so the exceptional set in $(\mathrm H_\infty)$ is empty for each $\delta>0$. That supplies the zero hypothesis for Theorems 1.1, 1.2 and 1.3. The written proofs use the applicable classical inputs (Z1)–(Z6) and the two zero-free facts following them in Section 2. In Lean, the fact that $\zeta$ has no zeros in $\Re s\ge1$ comes from Mathlib, and the release proves the absence of real zeros in $(0,1)$ from PrimeNumberTheoremAnd's Abel-summation formula.

Section 11 lists three steps taken outside Lean. # Part I. The arithmetic objects

## 1. Dirichlet series over pairs of primes

**Statement.** A Dirichlet series $\sum_n a_n n^{-s}$ converges absolutely in a half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s>\sigma_a$, and is holomorphic there. (See the $\Phi_2$ primer's section "Dirichlet series and half-planes of convergence" for the full statement and why only $\operatorname{Re}s$ matters.) What is new here is that the series are sums over *ordered pairs* of primes, so they are indexed by $(p,q)$ rather than by a single integer. Grouping by $N=p+q$ turns $G$ back into an ordinary Dirichlet series $\sum_N r(N)N^{-s}$, where $r(N)$ counts ordered prime pairs summing to $N$; likewise for $T$ with logarithmic weights.

The half-planes of absolute convergence the paper states: $\operatorname{Re}s>2$ for $G$, $T$ and $G_{\log}$, $\operatorname{Re}s>1$ for $G_2$, and $\operatorname{Re}s>\tfrac12$ for $S$.

**Worked example.** For $G$, the number of ordered pairs $(p,q)$ of primes with $p+q=N$ is at most $N$, so $r(N)\le N$ and $\sum_N r(N)N^{-\sigma}\le\sum_N N^{1-\sigma}$, which converges for $\sigma>2$. It diverges for $\sigma<2$. To see this, group the integers into dyadic blocks $2^j\le N<2^{j+1}$. By the prime number theorem the block sum $\sum_{2^j\le N<2^{j+1}}r(N)$ is of order $2^{2j}/j^2$: it is at most the square of the number of primes below $2^{j+1}$, and at least the square of the number of primes in $(2^{j-1},2^j)$, since two such primes have their sum in the block. (This is the precise sense of "$N$ has on average about $N/\log^2N$ representations"; it is a statement about averages over a block, not about each $N$.) So the block contributes about $2^{(2-\sigma)j}/j^2$ to $\sum_Nr(N)N^{-\sigma}$, which is unbounded in $j$ for $\sigma<2$. The abscissa is exactly $2$. $T$ and $G_{\log}$ have the same abscissa: the logarithmic weights change the block sums only by powers of $j$.

For $S$ the paper gives a short proof (Section 1.1). For any $t$ with $0\le t\le1$, $p^2+q^2\ge p^{2t}q^{2-2t}$. (This is the weighted arithmetic–geometric mean inequality: $tp^2+(1-t)q^2\ge(p^2)^t(q^2)^{1-t}$, and the left side is at most $p^2+q^2$.) So with $\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s$ the summand is at most $p^{1-\sigma}q^{2-\sigma}\cdot p^{-4t}q^{-4+4t}=p^{1-\sigma-4t}q^{-2-\sigma+4t}$ in absolute value. A double sum of a product $p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}$ is the product of two single sums, each convergent when its exponent is below $-1$. Here that asks for $2-\sigma<4t<1+\sigma$, and for $\sigma>\tfrac12$ there is such a $t$ in $[0,1]$. Try $\sigma=0.6$: take $4t=1.5$, and both exponents are $-1.1$.

For $G_2$, $p^2+q^2\ge2pq\ge pq$, so $\sum_{p,q}(p^2+q^2)^{-\sigma}\le\sum_{p,q}(pq)^{-\sigma}=P(\sigma)^2$, which is finite for $\sigma>1$.

**Where the paper uses it.** $G$, $T$ and $S$ are defined in the opening of Section 1.1, equation (1), and $G_2$ and $G_{\log}$ in an unnumbered display later in Section 1.1. The half-planes of convergence are stated there. The grouping and the representation count $r(N)$ underlie the sign check in Section 6, just before Lemma 6.2 ("an integer $n$ has on average about $n/\log^2n$ representations").

## 2. The prime zeta function $P$ and its natural boundary

**Statement.** The **prime zeta function** is
$$P(z)=\sum_p p^{-z},\qquad\operatorname{Re}z>1,$$
the sum over primes of $p^{-z}$. It is the prime-only analogue of $\zeta(s)=\sum_n n^{-s}$. It relates to $\zeta$ through the logarithm of the Euler product: since $\log\zeta(z)=\sum_p\sum_{k\ge1}\tfrac1k p^{-kz}=\sum_{k\ge1}\tfrac1k P(kz)$, Möbius inversion gives
$$P(z)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\mu(k)}k\log\zeta(kz).$$
This formula continues $P$ to the half-plane $\operatorname{Re}z>0$, but only as a **multivalued** function. Only squarefree $k$ occur, since $\mu(k)=0$ otherwise, and for such $k$ the term $\log\zeta(kz)$ has a logarithmic singularity wherever $\zeta(kz)$ has a zero or its pole, that is, at $z=1/k$ (from the pole of $\zeta$ at $1$) and at $z=\rho/k$ for every non-trivial zero $\rho$ of $\zeta$. These points are candidates for singularities of the sum, no more: several terms can be singular at one point, and their contributions might cancel (Topic 3). As $k$ grows the candidates crowd towards the line $\operatorname{Re}z=0$ from the right, but infinitely many candidates do not by themselves make a boundary. What is needed is genuine singularities near every point of the line, and that is the theorem of Landau and Walfisz: $\operatorname{Re}z=0$ is a **natural boundary** of $P$, so $P$ cannot be continued across any point of it. In Titchmarsh's account, the step that shows a candidate $\rho/p$ survives is a denominator argument, which the paper recalls in the remark closing Section 3: for $\operatorname{Re}\rho\ge\tfrac12$ and $p$ a prime larger than $m(\rho)$, the term $-m(\rho)/p$ is the only one in $c(\rho/p)$ whose denominator is divisible by $p$, so the others cannot cancel it.

Unlike a pole, a logarithmic singularity is not a meromorphic singularity: near such a point $P$ looks like $c\log(z-a)+(\text{holomorphic})$, which is many-valued. This is the main structural difference from the $\Phi_2$ paper, where the analogous object $\psi=-\zeta'/\zeta$ was meromorphic (poles, not logarithms).

**Worked example.** Take $k=1$ in the Möbius formula: the term $\mu(1)\log\zeta(z)=\log\zeta(z)$. Near the pole of $\zeta$ at $z=1$, $\zeta(z)\approx1/(z-1)$, so $\log\zeta(z)\approx-\log(z-1)$: a logarithmic singularity at $z=1$, with coefficient $-1$. Near a simple zero $\rho$ of $\zeta$, $\zeta(z)\approx\zeta'(\rho)(z-\rho)$, so $\log\zeta(z)\approx\log(z-\rho)+\text{const}$: a logarithmic singularity at $\rho$, coefficient $+1$. At a zero of multiplicity $m(\rho)$ the coefficient is $+m(\rho)$. The $k=2$ term $\mu(2)\log\zeta(2z)/2=-\tfrac12\log\zeta(2z)$ adds singularities at $z=1/2$ and at $z=\rho/2$, with the half from the $1/k$ factor and the sign from $\mu(2)=-1$. Stacking all $k$ gives the candidate set $\Sigma$ of the next topic.

**Where the paper uses it.** $P$ is defined at the start of Section 2.2. Its natural boundary (Landau–Walfisz) is quoted at the end of Section 1.1 and credited in Section 1.5, with pointers to Titchmarsh's and Dahlquist's accounts; the paper adds there that Kluyver had asserted it under RH, without details, and that Landau proved it under RH. $P$ reappears as the integrand of the Mellin–Barnes formula for $G$ and $S$ (Section 2.3, (K3)), and for $G_2$ and (with $Q$ as the other factor) $G_{\log}$ in Section 9, and is made single-valued as the branch $P^\kappa$ in Section 5.

## 3. $Q=-P'$, the candidate set $\Sigma$, and the coefficient $c(a)$

**Statement.** Differentiating $P$ term by term gives
$$Q(z)=-P'(z)=\sum_p(\log p)\,p^{-z},\qquad\operatorname{Re}z>1.$$
$Q$ is to $P$ as $\psi=-\zeta'/\zeta$ is to $\log\zeta$. Differentiating the Möbius formula (the $1/k$ cancels against the chain-rule $k$):
$$Q(z)=\sum_{k\ge1}\mu(k)\,\psi(kz),\qquad\psi=-\zeta'/\zeta.$$
Unlike $P$, $Q$ is **meromorphic** on $\operatorname{Re}z>0$: differentiation turns each $\log$ singularity into a simple pole. Its poles lie in the **candidate set**
$$\Sigma=\{1/k:k\ge1\text{ squarefree}\}\cup\{\rho/k:\rho\in\mathcal Z,\ k\ge1\text{ squarefree}\},$$
where $\mathcal Z$ is the set of non-trivial zeros. (Non-squarefree $k$ drop out because $\mu(k)=0$.) A point of $\Sigma$ can have **several representations** $\rho/k$. The residue of $Q$ at $a\in\Sigma$ is $R_a=-c(a)$, where
$$c(a)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\mu(k)}k\operatorname{ord}_{ka}\zeta,$$
and $\operatorname{ord}_w\zeta$ is the multiplicity $m(w)$ at a zero, $-1$ at $w=1$, and $0$ elsewhere. This sum is finite (only $k$ with $k\operatorname{Re}a\le1$ contribute). And $c(a)$ can **vanish**: several representations of one point contribute with the signs $\mu(k)$. So $\Sigma$ is only a set of candidates for the singularities; a point with $c(a)=0$ is not actually singular. Also $c(1)=-1$, and $c(a)$ is real with $c(\bar a)=c(a)$.

**Worked example.** At $a=1$: the only representation is $k=1$, $k\cdot1=1$ is the pole of $\zeta$, so $c(1)=\mu(1)\cdot(-1)=-1$ and $R_1=1$. (So $Q$ has a simple pole of residue $1$ at $z=1$, like $\psi$.) At a point $a=\rho/q$ with $q$ prime and $\rho$ the *only* representation: $c(a)=\mu(q)m(\rho)/q=-m(\rho)/q\ne0$. For an example of cancellation, suppose $u$ had two representations $\rho/1$ and $\rho'/2$ with $\rho,\rho'$ simple zeros; then $c(u)=\mu(1)\cdot1+\tfrac{\mu(2)}2\cdot1=1-\tfrac12=\tfrac12$, still nonzero but smaller than the first term; with more representations the sum could in principle reach zero. The paper's Section 3 is entirely about ruling cancellation out at enough points.

**Where the paper uses it.** The opening of Section 2.2 defines $Q$ and $\Sigma$. Equation (2) defines $c(a)$ and $R_a$. Lemma 2.1 gives their properties. The growth bound is Lemma 2.2: for each $\delta>0$ there are $C_\delta$ and a height $t_\delta$ with $|Q(w)|\le C_\delta\log|\operatorname{Im}w|$ for $\operatorname{Re}w\ge B_\infty+2\delta$ and $|\operatorname{Im}w|\ge t_\delta$. The formula $c(u)=\sum\mu(k)m(\rho')/k$ over representations opens Section 3.1, and the single-representation case $c(\rho/q)=-m(\rho)/q$ is the foundation of Theorem 3.3.

## 4. The hypothesis $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$, the quantities $B$ and $B_\infty$, and why RH implies it

**Statement.** Write $B=\sup_\rho\operatorname{Re}\rho$ and $B_\infty=\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{|\gamma|\ge t}\operatorname{Re}\rho$. $B$ is the supremum of the real parts of the non-trivial zeros; $B_\infty$ is the limit, as the height cutoff $t$ tends to infinity, of the supremum of the real parts of the zeros with $|\gamma|\ge t$. Neither supremum need be attained. Since $\rho$ and $1-\bar\rho$ are both zeros and there are infinitely many, $\tfrac12\le B_\infty\le B\le1$. The hypothesis of the paper,
$$(\mathrm{H}_\infty):\qquad\text{for every }\delta>0,\ \text{only finitely many }\rho\text{ have }\operatorname{Re}\rho>\tfrac12+\delta,$$
is exactly the statement $B_\infty=\tfrac12$. It allows any number of zeros off the critical line, as long as they do not persist to arbitrary height at a fixed distance to the right.

RH says every non-trivial zero has $\operatorname{Re}\rho=\tfrac12$, so under RH $B_\infty=B=\tfrac12$ and $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ holds trivially. It also follows from the statement that only finitely many zeros lie off the line, so it allows exceptions to RH. The paper cites Alpöge’s certified Lean proof of RH and uses the implication from RH to $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ in Corollary 1.4. The implication to the Lindelöf hypothesis is Backlund's criterion (Titchmarsh, Theorem 13.5). Under $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ one gets $B<1$ (a zero-free half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s>B$), because only finitely many zeros have $\operatorname{Re}\rho>\tfrac34$ and each of those has $\operatorname{Re}\rho<1$.

**Worked example.** Suppose, hypothetically, that $\zeta$ had exactly twelve zeros off the critical line (non-real off-line zeros come in orbits of four under $\rho\mapsto\bar\rho,\,1-\rho,\,1-\bar\rho$, so the number is a multiple of four), the rightmost at $\operatorname{Re}=0.7$, and all others on $\operatorname{Re}=\tfrac12$. Then $B=0.7$ and $B_\infty=\tfrac12$ (the twelve exceptions are a bounded set, so they do not affect the high-height supremum). $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ holds, RH fails. This is the regime the paper is built to survive: finitely many off-line zeros are tolerated, and they are exactly what create extra colliding pairs in Section 3.

**Where the paper uses it.** $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ is stated in Section 1.2, with the chain $\mathrm{RH}\Rightarrow(\mathrm{H}_\infty)\Rightarrow\mathrm{LH}$ and $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)\Rightarrow B<1$. $B$ and $B_\infty$ are defined there and again at the end of Section 2.1, where $B<1$ is shown. The arithmetic theorems (Theorem 3.1, and Theorem 9.4 for $G_2$) need only $B<1$; the half-plane statements (Theorems 1.1, 1.2, 1.3) need $B_\infty=\tfrac12$ to reach the boundary line (so that $\mathcal V_\infty=\mathcal V$). The paper says it does not show that $B<1$ alone gives continuation up to the boundary lines. A footnote in Section 1.2 mentions the zero-free half-plane preprints of OpenAI and Liu, both unrefereed, and says nothing in the paper uses either.

## 5. The classical inputs (Z1)–(Z6)

The paper cites six classical facts about $\zeta$ and $\Gamma$. The first three are the same ones the $\Phi_2$ paper used; see the $\Phi_2$ primer's section "The zeta function: continuation, zeros, RH, and the classical inputs" for their worked examples. I restate them briefly and teach (Z4), (Z5), (Z6), which are new here.

**(Z1) Symmetry of zeros.** If $\rho$ is a non-trivial zero then so are $\bar\rho$ and $1-\bar\rho$, with the same multiplicity (conjugation plus the functional equation). *(The $\Phi_2$ primer's zeta-function section.)* Used in Lemma 2.1 (reality of $c$) and Lemma 3.2 (the rigidity of a second representation).

**(Z2) Zero counting.** $N(t)=\frac t{2\pi}\log\frac t{2\pi}-\frac t{2\pi}+O(\log t)$, and locally $N(t+1)-N(t)\le C_1\log(t+3)$: a unit interval of heights holds $O(\log t)$ zeros. *(The $\Phi_2$ primer's zeta-function section.)* Used in Lemma 2.1(iii), Lemma 3.4, and the count in Theorem 3.3.

**(Z3) Local expansion of $\zeta'/\zeta$.** For $-1\le\operatorname{Re}z\le2$, $|\operatorname{Im}z|\ge2$ and $\zeta(z)\ne0$, $\zeta'/\zeta(z)$ differs from $\sum m(\rho)/(z-\rho)$, the sum over the distinct zeros with $|\gamma-\operatorname{Im}z|\le1$, by at most $C\log|\operatorname{Im}z|$. In words: in that strip, away from the zeros, $\zeta'/\zeta$ is a sum of pole terms from the zeros within height $1$, each weighted by its multiplicity, plus a logarithmic remainder. *(The $\Phi_2$ primer's zeta-function section.)* Used in the growth bound Lemma 2.2.

**(Z4) Hardy–Littlewood: positively many distinct zeros on the critical line.**

*Statement.* There are $A_0>0$ and $t_0$ with $N_0(t)\ge A_0 t$ for $t\ge t_0$, where $N_0(t)$ counts the **distinct** zeros $\tfrac12+i\gamma$ with $0<\gamma\le t$. In words: the critical line carries at least a linear number of zeros (the full count is of order $t\log t$), and they are distinct points, not one zero counted many times. The distinctness is a real subtlety: the printed wording of the theorems of Hardy and Littlewood does not state that the zeros are distinct. The proofs give it: they find the zeros in separate intervals, and a zero on a shared endpoint costs only a factor $2$ in $A_0$. The paper counts distinct zeros because Section 3 needs *many different* points $\rho/q$, and a zero counted $m$ times still gives one point; the bound $|\mathcal P(X)|\ge c_2X^2/\log^2X$ of Theorem 3.3(a) is stated with the distinct count.

*Worked example.* $N(t)\sim\frac t{2\pi}\log\frac t{2\pi}$ grows faster than linearly, so (Z4)'s linear lower bound $A_0t$ is far from capturing all zeros; it does not claim RH or even a positive proportion in the density sense of later work. It claims only that infinitely many distinct zeros sit on the line and that their count grows at least like $A_0t$. That is all the paper needs: it supplies, up to height $X$, at least $A_0X$ distinct critical-line zeros to build points $\rho/q$ from. The paper keeps those with $X/M<\gamma\le X$, where $M=\lceil2\log X/A_0\rceil$; at least $\tfrac12A_0X$ remain. Multiplied by the primes $q$ available for each kept zero ((Z5), Topic 16), these give $\gg X^2/\log^2X$ candidate points, which outnumber the $O(X\log X)$ zeros that could cause a second representation.

*Where used.* Theorem 3.3(a) (the lower bound $|\mathcal P(X)|\ge c_2X^2/\log^2X$), and the choice of $M=\lceil2\log X/A_0\rceil$ in Section 3.1. Theorem 9.4 reuses the same count for $G_2$.

**(Z5) Primes in ratio intervals (prime number theorem).**

*Statement.* For fixed $\theta>0$ and $x$ large, the open interval $(x,(1+\theta)x)$ contains at least $\theta x/(2\log x)$ primes. This is the prime number theorem in the form $\pi((1+\theta)x)-\pi(x)=\theta x/\log x+o(x/\log x)$: a relative window of width $\theta$ holds order $x/\log x$ primes.

*Worked example.* Take $\theta=1$, so the interval is $(x,2x)$. The PNT gives $\pi(2x)-\pi(x)\sim x/\log x$ (this is the quantitative Bertrand's postulate). For $x=10^6$ the estimate $x/\log x$ is about $72{,}000$, and the exact number of primes between $10^6$ and $2\cdot10^6$ is $148{,}933-78{,}498=70{,}435$. The lower bound of (Z5) is a separate matter: it says that from some $x_0(\theta)$ on the interval holds at least half the estimate, $x/(2\log x)$ here, and it names no value of $x_0$, so it asserts nothing about $x=10^6$ in particular. In the paper, the "ratio interval" is the set of primes $q$ with $\gamma/q$ in a fixed window $J$ around a target height $y$: $q$ ranges over an interval of relative length about $\varepsilon/y$, so (Z5) supplies $\gg\gamma/\log\gamma$ such primes per zero $\rho=\tfrac12+i\gamma$ once $\gamma$ is large.

*Where used.* Theorem 3.3(a): for each critical-line zero with $X/M<\gamma\le X$, (Z5) counts the admissible primes $q$ that place $u=\rho/q$ in the disc, giving $\gg X/\log^2X$ primes in the worst case, $\gamma$ about $X/M$. The lower cutoff matters: a fixed low zero has only a bounded number of primes in its window, however large $X$ is.

**(Z6) Stirling decay of $\Gamma$ on vertical strips.**

*Statement.* For $0<x_1\le x_2$ there is $C$ with $|\Gamma(x+iy)|\le C(1+|y|)^{x_2}e^{-\pi|y|/2}$ for $x\in[x_1,x_2]$ and all real $y$. The content is the exponential factor $e^{-\pi|y|/2}$: the Gamma function decays exponentially as you move up a vertical line, at rate $\pi/2$ per unit height. *(See the $\Phi_2$ primer's sections on the Gamma function and on Stirling on vertical strips for Stirling and the reflection formula behind this.)*

*Worked example.* On the line $\operatorname{Re}z=1$, $|\Gamma(1+iy)|=\sqrt{\pi y/\sinh\pi y}\approx\sqrt{2\pi\,|y|}\,e^{-\pi|y|/2}$ for large $|y|$, which matches the bound with $x_2=1$. The Beta kernel $\mathrm B(z,v-z)=\Gamma(z)\Gamma(v-z)/\Gamma(v)$ is therefore bounded, for $v$ in a compact set and $\operatorname{Re}z$ in a compact subset of $(0,\operatorname{Re}v)$, by a power of $1+|\operatorname{Im}z|$ times $e^{-\pi|\operatorname{Im}z|}$ (two Gamma factors, each contributing $e^{-\pi|y|/2}$). That bound is what makes the contour integrals of $G$ and $T$ converge and their far pairs negligible.

*Where used.* The kernel bound (K2) for $G$ (Section 2.3), and the far-pair estimate for $G$ in Lemma 3.4; for $G_2$, the kernel bound in Proposition 9.1 and the far-pair estimate in Lemma 9.3. The $S$-theorem does **not** use (Z6): its kernel is estimated elementarily, and Section 11 records that the formal theorem for $S$ does without it. $G_2$ and $G_{\log}$, like $G$, need (Z6) already for continuation, because their kernels are bounded through Stirling's formula (Section 11).

Two further standard facts are used without a Z-label: $\zeta(s)\ne0$ for $\operatorname{Re}s\ge1$ (Hadamard–de la Vallée Poussin on the line, the Euler product to its right), and $\zeta$ has no real zero in $(0,1)$. The Lean proofs get the first fact from Mathlib. PrimePairNB proves the second from PrimeNumberTheoremAnd's Abel-summation formula. Both facts are available without adding hypotheses to the formal boundary statements.

# Part II. The analytic toolkit

Most of this part is a pointer back to the $\Phi_2$ primer. The new items are the Beta/$S$ kernels and the Mellin–Barnes representation over pairs.

## 6. Holomorphic, meromorphic, identity theorem, residues

**Reminder.** A function is holomorphic where it is complex-differentiable; the **identity theorem** says two holomorphic functions on a connected open set that agree on a set with an accumulation point *in that connected open set* agree everywhere. (The accumulation point must lie in the domain; it need not lie in the set where the two functions agree. For example, $\sin(\pi/z)$ vanishes at $z=1/n$, which accumulate at $0$, yet it is not zero on $\operatorname{Re}z>0$.) A function is meromorphic if locally $(z-a)^n g(z)$ with $g$ holomorphic and $n\in\mathbb Z$; a simple pole has $\operatorname{Res}_{z=a}f=\lim_{z\to a}(z-a)f(z)$. *(See the $\Phi_2$ primer's sections "Holomorphic functions and the identity theorem" and "Meromorphic functions, poles, order, and residues" for statements, the connectedness caveat, and worked examples.)*

**Where the paper uses it.** The identity theorem glues the local pieces of the $T$-continuation (Theorem 4.1, Step 4) and is used in the slit argument for $G$ and $S$ (Lemma 8.1), which Section 9.1 also applies to $G_2$. A proposed extension that agrees with a branch near one point must agree with it throughout the slit punctured disc, where the branch is defined. The jump across the slit then gives a contradiction. Lemma 9.10 gives the corresponding slit argument for $G_{\log}$. For $T$, poles accumulate at each point of the boundary line, as stated in Theorem 1.1 and proved in Section 4. Residues appear as $R_a=\operatorname{Res}_{z=a}Q$ throughout, and as the residue of $T$ at a candidate point (Theorem 4.2).

## 7. The Gamma function, the Beta kernel, and the $S$-kernel

**Statement.** The Gamma function $\Gamma$ extends the factorial ($\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!$), satisfies $\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)$ and the reflection formula $\Gamma(w)\Gamma(1-w)=\pi/\sin\pi w$, and decays up vertical lines by (Z6). *(See the $\Phi_2$ primer's section on the Gamma function: recurrence, no zeros, conjugation, reflection, decay.)* New here are two kernels built from $\Gamma$:

- The **Beta kernel** of $G$ and $T$: $\mathrm B(a,b)=\dfrac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}$. As a function of the contour variable it is $\mathcal K(z,v)=\mathrm B(z,v-z)=\Gamma(z)\Gamma(v-z)/\Gamma(v)$.
- The **$S$-kernel**: $K(w)=\Gamma(w)\Gamma(2-w)$, which by reflection equals $(1-w)\pi/\sin\pi w$. In the contour variable it enters as $\mathcal K(z,v)=\tfrac12 K\big(\tfrac{2z-v+3}4\big)$.

Section 9 adds a third kernel, for $G_2$: the Beta kernel with halved arguments and a factor $\tfrac12$, $\mathcal K(z,v)=\Gamma(z/2)\Gamma((v-z)/2)/(2\Gamma(v/2))$. Topic 17 covers it.

The Beta function is holomorphic where its arguments have positive real part; $K(w)=(1-w)\pi/\sin\pi w$ is holomorphic on $0<\operatorname{Re}w<2$ (its poles are the integers, cancelled at $w=1$ by the factor $1-w$).

**Worked example.** $\mathrm B(1,1)=\Gamma(1)\Gamma(1)/\Gamma(2)=1\cdot1/1=1$; $\mathrm B(2,1)=\Gamma(2)\Gamma(1)/\Gamma(3)=1\cdot1/2=\tfrac12$. For the $S$-kernel, $K(1)=\Gamma(1)\Gamma(1)=1$, and the reflection form gives $K(1-x)=\pi x/\sin\pi x$, $K(\tfrac12+x)=(\tfrac12-x)\pi/\cos\pi x$, the two pieces whose sum Lemma 2.4 shows is nonzero off the real axis. As a sanity check of the formulas, take $x=\tfrac14$, that is $u=4x=1$ and $t=0$ in the paper's proof: $K(\tfrac34)=\dfrac{\pi/4}{\sin(\pi/4)}=\dfrac{\pi}{2\sqrt2}$ and $K(\tfrac34)=\dfrac{\pi/4}{\cos(\pi/4)}=\dfrac{\pi}{2\sqrt2}$ from the two reflection forms, so the bracket is $\pi/\sqrt2\approx2.22144$. This agrees with the paper's combined formula, whose numerator is $\pi\cdot\tfrac12/\sqrt2$ and denominator $\sin\tfrac\pi4\cos\tfrac\pi4=\tfrac12$. The point $u=1$ is real, so it is outside the lemma, which concerns non-real $u$.

**Where the paper uses it.** The two kernels are defined in Section 2.3. $\mathrm B(a,b)$ is the weight in the residue of $T$ (Theorem 4.2) and in $X_x'(x)$ for $G$ (equation (7), labelled `eq:Xprime`). $K$ is the corresponding weight for $S$. Lemma 2.4 is the non-vanishing of the $S$ main-term bracket. The kernel of $G_2$ is Proposition 9.1, and its weight $\mathrm B(a/2,b/2)$ is in Lemma 9.2.

## 8. The Mellin–Barnes representation

**Statement.** A **Mellin–Barnes integral** writes a function as a vertical contour integral of a product of Gamma factors against a power. The building block the paper uses is, for $x>0$ and $0<c<\operatorname{Re}s$,
$$(1+x)^{-s}=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\frac{\Gamma(z)\Gamma(s-z)}{\Gamma(s)}\,x^{-z}\,dz,$$
and for the $S$-series the case $s=2$: $(1+x)^{-2}=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{(d)}K(w)x^{-w}\,dw$, $0<d<2$. Applying this to $(p+q)^{-s}=p^{-s}(1+q/p)^{-s}$ and summing over primes turns each series into a single contour integral of $\mathcal K$ against $P(z)P(v-z)$ (for $G,S$) or $Q(w)Q(s-w)$ (for $T$). The three facts the paper abstracts are:

- (K1) $\mathcal K$ is holomorphic in the pair $(z,v)$ on the set where $v\in\mathcal V$ and $0<\operatorname{Re}z<\operatorname{Re}v$. Here $\mathcal V=\{\operatorname{Re}v>1\}$ for $G$ and $\{1<\operatorname{Re}v<3\}$ for $S$.
- (K2) for $v$ in a compact subset of $\mathcal V$ and $\operatorname{Re}z$ in a compact subset of $(0,\operatorname{Re}v)$, $|\mathcal K(z,v)|$ is at most a constant times $(1+|\operatorname{Im}z|)^A e^{-\pi|\operatorname{Im}z|/2}$ (for $G$ with the faster rate $e^{-\pi|\operatorname{Im}z|}$). This is an upper bound, uniform on those ranges, with a polynomial factor; the exponential is the rate in the bound.
- (K3) the paper states the representation $D(v)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\mathcal K(z,v)P(z)P(v-z)\,dz$ for $v\in\mathcal V$ with $\operatorname{Re}v>2$ (so $2<\operatorname{Re}v<3$ for $S$) and $1<c<\operatorname{Re}v-1$.

Fubini justifies interchanging the prime sum and the integral, with majorant $P(c)P(\operatorname{Re}v-c)$ times the kernel bound. *(See the $\Phi_2$ primer's section "The Mellin transform, and swapping sums with integrals (Tonelli and Fubini)" for Mellin transforms and the sum–integral interchange.)*

**Worked example.** The reason the candidate points are **sums** $a+b$: in $D(v)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int \mathcal K(z,v)P(z)P(v-z)\,dz$, the first factor $P(z)$ is singular at the left points $z=a\in\Sigma$ and the second $P(v-z)$ at the right points $z=v-b$, $b\in\Sigma$. For generic $v$ the contour slips between them. The two can only *collide*, trapping the contour, when some right point meets some left point, $a=v-b$, i.e. $v=a+b$. That is why, inside $\mathcal V_\infty$ (the part of $\mathcal V$ to the right of $2B_\infty$, which is all of $\mathcal V$ under $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$), $D$ can be singular only at points $v\in\Sigma+\Sigma$. This is a statement about the interior of the domain, where these sums are the candidate points. It says nothing about the boundary line, where the theorems show that no branch continues across any point.

**Where the paper uses it.** The two kernels and (K1)–(K3) are Section 2.3; (K3) is equation (3), and equation (4) is the version for $T$. The Mellin–Barnes step for $G$ cites Egami–Matsumoto's formula (1.4); the $S$ version is derived in Section 2.3 with the change of variable $z=2w+s-1$. The same formula gives the integrals for $G_2$ (Proposition 9.1, with $z=2w$) and for $G_{\log}$ (Lemma 9.5). Section 2.4 identifies where a left and a right candidate point meet. For the continuations in Theorem 7.4, singularities in $\mathcal V_\infty$ can occur only at sums $v=a+b$.

## 9. Cauchy's theorem, the residue theorem, and contour shifts

**Reminder.** On a region where $f$ is holomorphic, a contour integral is unchanged when the contour is deformed with its endpoints fixed. The contours of this paper are infinite and have no endpoints to fix, so each deformation is done at finite height and the connecting segments at the top and bottom are shown to contribute nothing in the limit. Moving a contour across a pole changes the integral by $2\pi i$ times the residue, with the sign set by the orientation of the loop that the old and new contours form (plus when it runs counterclockwise round the pole). *(See the $\Phi_2$ primer's section "Cauchy's theorem, the residue theorem, and contour shifts".)* Holomorphy of an integral depending on a parameter, by differentiating under the integral sign when the integrand is dominated, is the $\Phi_2$ primer's section "Holomorphy of integrals depending on a parameter".

**Where the paper uses it.** The whole continuation of $T$ (Section 4) is contour-shifting: moving the line $\operatorname{Re}w=c_0$ left to $\operatorname{Re}w=c$ across finitely many poles leaves residues, which assemble into the pole structure; the horizontal segments at height $h$ are bounded by Lemma 2.2 and the decay of the kernel (Theorem 4.1, Step 1). Independence of the sheet $D^\kappa$ from the choice of separating graph (Theorem 5.3) is Cauchy's theorem applied to a homotopy of graphs, with the connecting segments at large height vanishing by (K2) and Lemma 5.1(b). Holomorphy of $D^\kappa$ in $v$ (Theorem 5.3) and of the outer integral $I_{\mathrm{out}}$ (Theorem 6.1) is differentiation under the integral.

## 10. Natural boundaries: the single-valued case

**Statement.** A function holomorphic on a half-plane has the boundary line as a **natural boundary** if it cannot be continued across any point of the line. The simplest sufficient condition, and the one that applies to $T$: if the continuation is meromorphic and its poles accumulate at every point of the line, no meromorphic function on a disc straddling the line can agree with it on the inside, because that function would have poles accumulating at an interior point of its own domain, which is impossible. *(See the $\Phi_2$ primer's section "Natural boundaries, blow-up, and accumulating interior poles" for the definition and the subtlety of the meromorphic vs. holomorphic phrasing.)*

**Worked example.** The lacunary series $\sum_n z^{2^n}$ is the textbook natural boundary on the unit circle, by density of singular points. For a *pole*-accumulation boundary, imagine a function meromorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s>1$ with a simple pole at every point $1+1/n$, $n\ge1$. These poles lie inside the half-plane and accumulate at $1$. A function meromorphic on a neighbourhood of $1$ that agreed with it on the part of the neighbourhood to the right of the line would have the same poles there, and they would pile up at $1$, an interior point of its domain, which is impossible. Theorem 1.1 realizes exactly this for $T$ along the whole line $\operatorname{Re}s=1$.

**Where the paper uses it.** Theorem 1.1 for $T$. The multivalued case ($G$, $S$, $G_2$, $G_{\log}$) needs the stronger machinery of Part III, because these series have no poles to accumulate; their singularities are branch points.

# Part III. Multivaluedness: the heart of the paper

This is the new complex analysis. The $\Phi_2$ paper never left the world of single-valued meromorphic functions. Here $G$ and $S$ are many-valued, so the paper has to say *which value* (which branch) it means, and prove the boundary holds no matter which. Read this part slowly.

## 11. Logarithmic singularities, branches, and the jump of $\operatorname{Log}$ across a cut

**Statement.** The complex logarithm $\operatorname{Log}z$ cannot be defined continuously on a punctured disc around $0$: going once counterclockwise around $0$, its imaginary part (the argument) increases by $2\pi$, so $\operatorname{Log}$ returns with $2\pi i$ added. To make it single-valued one makes a **cut**, removing a ray from $0$ to infinity, and defines $\operatorname{Log}$ on the slit plane. Across the cut, $\operatorname{Log}$ **jumps by $2\pi i$**: the limits from the two sides differ by exactly $2\pi i$. A function of the form $c\log(z-a)+(\text{holomorphic})$ near $a$, with $c\ne0$, has a **logarithmic singularity** (a **branch point**) at $a$, and the constant $c$ is its coefficient, recoverable as $\frac1{2\pi i}\times(\text{the jump across the cut})$. If $c=0$ there is no singularity. In the paper the coefficient is a holomorphic function $X_x(v)$, not a constant, and the condition for a branch point is that $X_x$ is not identically zero near $x$.

The paper needs a logarithm aligned with a chosen cut direction. It writes $\operatorname{Log}_\kappa(y)=i\theta+\operatorname{Log}(y/e)$, where $e=e^{i\theta}$ is a fixed unit direction (slope $\kappa$) and the cut runs along the ray $y\in-e[0,\infty)$. On $\operatorname{Re}y>0$ this agrees with the principal logarithm.

**Worked example.** Principal $\operatorname{Log}$ has its cut on the negative real axis. Just above it, at $z=-1+i0^+$, $\operatorname{Log}z=i\pi$; just below, at $z=-1-i0^+$, $\operatorname{Log}z=-i\pi$. The jump is $i\pi-(-i\pi)=2\pi i$. Now a concrete branch point: $f(z)=3\log(z-2)$ near $z=2$. Circling $z=2$ once counterclockwise adds $2\pi i$ to $\log(z-2)$, so $f$ changes by $3\cdot2\pi i=6\pi i$; the coefficient $3$ is $6\pi i/(2\pi i)$. This is exactly how the paper reads off the coefficient $X_x$ (Lemma 8.1): across the slit, the holomorphic part is continuous, so the whole jump of the branch is $2\pi i\,X_x$.

**Where the paper uses it.** $\operatorname{Log}_\kappa$ and its cut are defined in Section 5.1 (the unnumbered definition of $\operatorname{Log}_\kappa$). The local model $D^\kappa(v)=\operatorname{Log}_\kappa(v-x)X_x(v)+H(v)$ (Theorem 6.1) is a logarithmic singularity with coefficient $X_x$. The jump-by-$2\pi i$ is the mechanism of the monodromy jump (Lemma 7.2) and of the obstruction (Lemma 8.1): a single-valued meromorphic function cannot have the jump unless $X_x\equiv0$.

## 12. Germs, analytic continuation along a path, and monodromy

**Statement.** A **germ** of a function at a point $s_1$ is the function considered only very near $s_1$: formally, its convergent power series there, or equivalently the function up to agreement on some small disc. **Analytic continuation along a path** $\gamma$ from $s_0$ to $s_1$ means a chain of overlapping discs along $\gamma$, with a holomorphic function on each, agreeing on the overlaps; the germ you end with at $s_1$ depends, in general, on the path. When two paths with the same endpoints give different germs, the function has non-trivial **monodromy**: continuing around a closed loop can return a different germ. A branch point is the local source of monodromy.

For this paper, a germ of $G$ at $s_1$ is **admissible** if it is reached from the region $\operatorname{Re}s>2$ (where $G$ is the convergent series) by continuing along a path that stays in $\{\operatorname{Re}s>1\}$ and avoids the catalogue $\mathcal C_G$; a germ of $S$ is admissible if it is reached from $\operatorname{Re}s>1$ (not merely from the half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s>\tfrac12$ where the series converges) along a path in $\{\operatorname{Re}s>0\}\setminus\mathcal C_S$ along which continuation exists. For $G_2$ the path starts in $\operatorname{Re}s>1$ and stays in $\{\operatorname{Re}s>\tfrac12\}$ off the catalogue $\mathcal C_2=\{s:\operatorname{Re}s>\tfrac12,\ 2s\in\Pi\}$; for $G_{\log}$ the definition is the one for $G$, with the same catalogue $\mathcal C_G$. "Extends meromorphically to an open set $W$" means some function meromorphic on $W$ agrees with the germ near $s_1$.

**Worked example.** The archetype is $\log z$ itself. Start with the principal branch near $z=1$ (germ: $\log z=\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^{n-1}(z-1)^n/n$). Continue it counterclockwise around $0$ back to $z=1$: the germ you return with is $\log z+2\pi i$, a *different* germ at the same point. That is monodromy, and $0$ is the branch point producing it. The function has infinitely many germs at $z=1$, one per winding number. The paper's $G$ behaves the same way around each candidate point $x$: one small counterclockwise loop around $x$, enclosing no other candidate point, adds $2\pi i\,X_x$ to the germ (Theorem 6.1 and Theorem 7.4(c)), so if $X_x\not\equiv0$ the germ changes.

**Where the paper uses it.** Admissible germs are defined in Section 1.3. The germs reached along the paths of Theorem 7.4 ("Every branch") are contained in a family. A germ reached at an endpoint in the sheet domain is a sheet $D^\kappa$ plus an integer combination $m$ of the monodromy functions $M_x$, plus a constant $C$ for $S$. The paths avoid the catalogue in $\operatorname{Re}v>2B_\infty$. For $G$ they start in $\operatorname{Re}v>2$. For $S$ they start in $\operatorname{Re}v>3$, continuation must exist along the path, and the endpoint must lie in the sheet strip $2B_\infty<\operatorname{Re}v<3$. A path for $S$ may leave that strip on the right before returning. Under $(\mathrm H_\infty)$, Corollary 8.3 makes a germ reached by any arrival path of continuation admissible if the endpoint is off the catalogue. At endpoints in the sheet domain, the same family then applies. The paper leaves open which choices of $m$ and $C$ are reached. The monodromy jump is Lemma 7.2 and Theorem 7.3. Admissible germs of $G_2$ and $G_{\log}$ are defined before Theorem 1.3, and their branches are described in Section 9 (for $G_{\log}$, Theorem 9.8).

## 13. Hadamard and Borel's cut-and-contour construction: the sheet $D^\kappa$

**Statement.** To integrate a multivalued integrand without chasing the contour along paths, the paper **fixes a direction and cuts**. Choose a generic slope $\kappa$ (so no two distinct points of $\Sigma$, and no two distinct points of $\Pi=\Sigma+\Sigma$, share a $\kappa$-line). Cut the half-plane along parallel westward rays from every point of $\Sigma$. On the complement $U_\kappa$, the primitive of $-Q$ that equals $P$ on $\operatorname{Re}z>1$ is single-valued; call it $P^\kappa$. Near a singular point $a$ it is $c(a)\operatorname{Log}_\kappa(z-a)+(\text{holomorphic})$, and it grows no faster than $Q$ at large height. Then, cutting the $v$-plane along westward rays from the points $x\in\Sigma+\Sigma$, the paper defines the **sheet**
$$D^\kappa(v)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma\mathcal K(z,v)\,P^\kappa(z)\,P^\kappa(v-z)\,dz$$
over a **separating graph** $\Gamma$: a contour that passes, on each $\kappa$-line, east of every left point $a$ and west of every right point $v-b$. The value does not depend on $\Gamma$, is holomorphic in $v$ off the cuts, and equals the series where the series converges. This single contour with a fixed integrand *is* one branch of $G$ (or $S$). Rotating $\kappa$ gives the rule for passing from one sheet to another, and every germ reached along a path is a sheet plus an accumulated correction (Topic 15).

This is the additive, infinite-contour form of a classical construction. The classical setting is a contour integral with the multiplicative kernel $dz/z$, of which the Hadamard product of two power series is the standard case: the integral is continued by a contour that separates the singular points of one factor from those of the other. The paper credits separating contours for that kernel to Hadamard and Borel, and cut domains, for finitely many singular points, to Hadamard; Borel has the contour deformation without the cuts. For Hadamard products it cites Pérez-Marco. The paper adapts these to an additive kernel integral over an infinite contour, with singular points that accumulate at the line.

**Worked example.** Why a cut makes a multivalued function single-valued: $\log z$ on the plane cut along the negative real axis is a genuine function (the principal branch): the cut blocks the loop that would have changed its value. The sheet $D^\kappa$ is the same trick one dimension up: the cuts $L_x$ block exactly the loops that would move the contour across a collision, so the integral is well-defined. The direction $\kappa$ must be a genuine slope, not horizontal ($\kappa=0$): the points $1/k$ all lie on the real axis, and an off-line zero $\rho$ and its mirror $1-\bar\rho$ share an imaginary part, so a horizontal cut would stack singular points on one line and break genericity.

**Where the paper uses it.** $P^\kappa$ is Lemma 5.1; the separating graph and its existence are Lemma 5.2; the sheet $D^\kappa$ and its three properties (graph-independence, holomorphy, equals the series) are Theorem 5.3, equation (11) (`eq:Dk`). The credit to Hadamard and Borel is in Sections 1.5 and 5.

## 14. Pinch (Picard–Lefschetz) singularities and the local logarithm

**Statement.** When the contour of an integral is trapped ("pinched") between two singular points of the integrand that approach each other, the integral can develop a singularity at the moment of collision. For two logarithmic singular points colliding, the pinch produces a **logarithmic** term in the external variable, with coefficient the product of the two local coefficients times an integral of the kernel between the two colliding points. A collision gives a candidate, not yet a singularity: several collisions can occur at one point, their terms add, and if the total coefficient $X_x$ vanishes identically the local formula is holomorphic there. The criterion the local model supplies for a genuine singularity is $X_x\not\equiv0$. This is the local content of the Picard–Lefschetz / Pham theory of pinch (vanishing-cycle) singularities, specialized here to a one-dimensional arc.

Concretely, near a collision the paper writes the two factors as explicit logarithms, $P^\kappa(z)=c(a)\operatorname{Log}t+E_1$ and $P^\kappa(v-z)=c(b)\operatorname{Log}(\epsilon-t)+E_2$ (local coordinate $t$, with $\epsilon$ measuring how far the right point has moved from the left). The only term that can be singular is the product of the two logarithms, and a one-variable calculation gives
$$\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{A_\epsilon}f(t)\operatorname{Log}t\,\operatorname{Log}(\epsilon-t)\,dt=\operatorname{Log}\epsilon\cdot J_{ab}(v)+(\text{holomorphic}),$$
where $J_{ab}(v)=\int_a^{v-b}\mathcal K(z,v)\,dz$. Summing over all colliding pairs at $x$ gives the local model
$$D^\kappa(v)=\operatorname{Log}_\kappa(v-x)\,X_x(v)+H(v),\qquad X_x(v)=\sum_{a+b=x}c(a)c(b)\,J_{ab}(v),$$
with $H$ holomorphic. The coefficient $X_x$ is holomorphic on $\mathcal V$ (the half-plane $\operatorname{Re}v>1$ for $G$, the strip $1<\operatorname{Re}v<3$ for $S$), vanishes at $x$ (so, when $X_x'(x)\ne0$, the singularity is $\delta\log\delta$, not $\log\delta$), and its derivative at the collision is the arithmetic sum of Section 3:
$$X_x'(x)=\sum_{a+b=x}c(a)c(b)\,\mathrm B(a,b)\quad(G),\qquad X_x'(x)=\tfrac12\sum_{a+b=x}c(a)c(b)\,K\big(\tfrac{a-b+3}4\big)\quad(S).$$

**Worked example (how a colliding singularity makes a logarithm).** Take the elementary integral
$$f(\epsilon)=\int_0^1\frac{dt}{t+\epsilon}=\operatorname{Log}(1+\epsilon)-\operatorname{Log}\epsilon.$$
As $\epsilon\to0^+$ the pole of the integrand at $t=-\epsilon$ slides into the endpoint $t=0$ of the contour: the contour is pinched at its end, and $f(\epsilon)=-\operatorname{Log}\epsilon+(\text{holomorphic})$ blows up logarithmically. The logarithm is born exactly from the collision of a singular point with the contour. The paper's pinch is the two-logarithm version of this, with the coefficient $J_{ab}$ coming from the kernel integrated between the colliding points.

A second check, the sign, at $x=2$ for $G$: the only pair is $(1,1)$, $c(1)^2=1$, and the leading term is $+\delta\log\delta$. This matches the paper's prime number theorem heuristic, which is an averaged statement: $n$ has on average about $n/\log^2n$ ordered representations as a sum of two primes, so $G(2+\delta)$ behaves like $\sum_n n^{-1-\delta}\log^{-2}n$, which (set $n=e^y$) behaves like $\int_1^\infty e^{-\delta y}y^{-2}\,dy=1+\delta\log\delta+O(\delta)$, whose singular term is $+\delta\log\delta$. The paper says the heuristic serves only as a check of the sign and the normalization, and that no proof uses it.

**Where the paper uses it.** The local model is Theorem 6.1, equation (12) (`eq:local`); the figure in Section 6 shows the outer contour and the arcs. $J_{ab}$ and $X_x$ are defined in Section 2.4, equations (5)–(7) (`eq:Jab`, `eq:Xx`, `eq:Xprime`). The pinch calculation cites Pham and Pérez-Marco (Sections 1.5 and 6). Lemma 6.2, at the end of Section 6, is the version in which one of the two factors has a pole where the other has a logarithm; the paper compares it with the mixed case of Pham's formula, and Topic 17 covers it.

## 15. Rotation, monodromy, and "every branch has the same $X_x$"

**Statement.** A sheet $D^\kappa$ is *one* branch, depending on the cut direction $\kappa$. Rotation is the rule for passing between fixed sheets. Turning $\kappa$ sweeps the cuts across the base point. Each time the cut from $x$ crosses the base point, the sheet jumps by the function $M_x=2\pi i\,X_x$. Theorem 7.3 gives the difference between two sheets at one base point as a sum of these functions. Every $M_x$ is **holomorphic on the whole of $\mathcal V$** (the half-plane for $G$, the strip $1<\operatorname{Re}v<3$ for $S$) (because each $J_{ab}$ is), so a jump by an integer combination of the $M_x$ preserves the local coefficient. Theorem 7.4(c) gives the same coefficient $X_x$ for every germ of the sheet family near $x$ and off its cut. The family contains the germs reached at endpoints in the sheet domain along the paths of Theorem 7.4(a) and (b). These paths stay in $\operatorname{Re}v>2B_\infty$ and avoid the catalogue. For $G$ the paths start in $\operatorname{Re}v>2$. For $S$ they start in $\operatorname{Re}v>3$ and may make excursions to the right of the sheet strip. The statement for $S$ requires continuation to exist along the path. Under $(\mathrm H_\infty)$, Corollary 8.3 gives admissibility for any arrival path of continuation ending off the catalogue, so the same description holds at endpoints in the sheet domain. Each germ in the family is a sheet plus an integer combination of the $M_x$ plus a constant $C$. Near a candidate point $x$ and off the cut for a generic direction, its local form is $\operatorname{Log}_\kappa(v-x)X_x+(\text{holomorphic})$. A path can change sheet many times, and the integer combination is the accumulated correction. The constant is $0$ for $G$. An excursion of the path to the right of the strip for $S$ can add a nonzero constant. The paper computes one case in which a loop adds $4\pi^2$.

This is what lets the boundary argument work "on every branch": whatever branch you are on, the coefficient at $x$ is $X_x$, and if $X_x\not\equiv0$ no single-valued meromorphic function can match it across the line.

**Worked example.** Return to $\log z$. Its branches are $\log z+2\pi i n$, $n\in\mathbb Z$. They differ by the *constant* $2\pi i n$, a function with no singularity, so every branch has the same local behaviour $\log(z-0)$ at the branch point $0$: the same coefficient $1$. The paper's $M_x$ play the role of $2\pi i$: holomorphic jumps that reshuffle branches without touching any coefficient. Section 2.4 defines $M_x$ as $2\pi iX_x$ and proves it is holomorphic *throughout* $\mathcal V$. Since $X_x(x)=0$, the jump function $M_x$ is nonconstant whenever $X_x$ is not identically zero. If $X_x$ is identically zero, the jump is zero. Holomorphy throughout $\mathcal V$ is what lets the paper conclude that changing sheets preserves the local coefficient.

**Where the paper uses it.** The constancy between critical directions is Lemma 7.1; the jump across a critical direction is Lemma 7.2; the full rotation identity is Theorem 7.3; "every branch" and the common coefficient are Theorem 7.4. The constant $C$ appears only for $S$, because an $S$-path can leave the strip $1<\operatorname{Re}v<3$ where the sheets are defined, and return. Theorem 7.4(b) tracks it by a derivative argument: on the excursion the correction $m$ is continued outside the strip, where its derivative is meromorphic and can have poles (at $s=a+1$ and $s=b+2$ for a term $J_{ab}$), so on re-entry the correction can differ from $m$ by a constant.

# Part IV. Putting the arithmetic to work

Section 7 explains the excursion by tracking a derivative. On the right of the strip the convergent series $D$ is still holomorphic. The correction to $D$ leaves as $h=m+C$, whose derivative $m'$ is meromorphic on the whole plane. If the germ continues, subtracting $D$ along the excursion continues $h$. Uniqueness of continuation fixes the derivative of that correction to $m'$, so on re-entry the correction differs from $h$ by a constant. This is the source of the $C$ allowed in Theorem 7.4(b).

## 16. The non-cancellation argument

**Statement.** The arithmetic theorem (Theorem 3.1) is: assuming $B<1$, every disc around a point of the line contains infinitely many points $x=1+\rho/q$ (with $\rho$ a critical-line zero and $q$ prime) at which both pair-sums $\Theta_G(x)$ and $\Theta_S(x)$ are nonzero. Under $B<1$ alone a point might have infinitely many pairs, so part of the statement is that both sums converge absolutely. The coefficient $X_x$ of the local model is defined only for $x\in\mathcal X$, that is for candidate points with $\operatorname{Re}x>2B_\infty$, and for those $x$ the paper has $\Theta_G(x)=X_x'(x)$ for $G$ and $\Theta_S(x)=2X_x'(x)$ for $S$ (derivative in $v$). Under $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$, where $2B_\infty=1$, every point of the theorem is in $\mathcal X$; under $B<1$ alone it need not be. The proof is a dominant-term argument (see the $\Phi_2$ primer's section "The head–tail principle: a dominant term cannot be cancelled") in three steps:

1. **Single representations.** Choose $x=1+\rho/q$ so that $\rho/q$ has a *single* representation; then $c(\rho/q)=-m(\rho)/q$ is one clean term, with the lower bound $|c(u)/u|\ge1/(X+1)$. A second representation would force a zero off the critical line with a prescribed denominator (Lemma 3.2, "rigid"), and such zeros are counted by $N$, so they are rare: at most $O(X\log X)$ of the $\gg X^2/\log^2X$ available pairs are spoiled (Theorem 3.3).
2. **Near pairs.** A competing pair of height $\le H=2\log X$ is one of only $\log^{O(1)}X$ possibilities, so the few points where one occurs are discarded, far fewer than the points available. The same $H$ serves both kernels.
3. **Far pairs.** Pairs above height $H$ contribute at most $e^{-H}=X^{-2}$ in total, by the decay of the kernels (Lemma 3.4: (Z6) for the Beta kernel of $G$, and for $S$ an elementary bound from $K(w)=(1-w)\pi/\sin\pi w$, with no use of (Z6)), which is smaller than the main term of size $\approx1/X$. Exact coincidences above $H$ are allowed in any number: a sum smaller in modulus than the main term cannot cancel it.

The point of taking the critical line is that it supplies both the *single-term* lower bound $|c(u)|\ge1/q$ and the *abundance* of points, so the count of near pairs can stand in for the hypothesis on additive relations among ordinates used in earlier work.

**Worked example.** Count the orders of magnitude at height $\sim X$. Distinct critical-line zeros up to $X$: $\ge A_0X$ by (Z4). The paper keeps only those with $X/M<\gamma\le X$, where $M=\lceil2\log X/A_0\rceil$, and at least $\tfrac12A_0X$ remain. The cutoff is needed because the next bound fails for low zeros: a fixed zero has only a bounded number of primes in its window, however large $X$ is. Admissible primes $q$ for each kept zero: $\gg X/\log^2X$ by (Z5). Product: $\gg X^2/\log^2X$ candidate pairs $(\rho,q)$. Pairs spoiled by a second representation: $\le N(2X)\le2X\log(2X)$, times $2$. Points discarded for a near pair: $\ll(\log X\log\log X)^2$. Both spoilers are $o(X^2/\log^2X)$, so infinitely many good points survive as $X\to\infty$. The main term $-2c(u)/u$ has modulus $\ge2/(X+1)$; the far pairs total $\le X^{-2}$; for large $X$ the first beats the second, so the sum is nonzero.

**Where the paper uses it.** Theorem 3.1 (statement), Lemma 3.2 (rigidity of a second representation), Theorem 3.3 (the count), Lemma 3.4 (far pairs), and the assembly in Section 3.3. The remark closing Section 3 explains why single representations, not mere non-vanishing, are needed; it places the denominator step that gives non-vanishing in Titchmarsh's account of the Landau–Walfisz theorem. Theorem 3.3 itself assumes neither $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ nor $B<1$: the zeros that spoil a pair may lie arbitrarily close to the critical line, and the count uses only the total $N(2X)$. The proof of Theorem 1.1 uses Theorem 4.2 to turn $\Theta_G\ne0$ into poles of $T$. The proof of Theorem 1.2 uses $\Theta_G$ and $\Theta_S$ to meet the derivative condition in Lemma 8.1. The proof of Theorem 1.3(b) for $G_{\log}$ uses Lemma 9.9 to turn $\Theta_G\ne0$ into the value condition in Lemma 9.10. For $G_2$ the argument is run again with a new sum $\Theta_2$ (Theorem 9.4; Topic 17).

## 17. Two more kernels: halved Gamma arguments, and a logarithm meeting a pole

**Statement.** Section 9 treats two further series by changing one layer of the argument for each.

*The squares, $G_2$.* Put $v=2s$ and $D(v)=G_2(v/2)$. The Mellin–Barnes formula of Topic 8, applied with $x=(p/q)^2$ and the change of variable $z=2w$, gives the same integral with the kernel
$$\mathcal K(z,v)=\frac{\Gamma(z/2)\,\Gamma((v-z)/2)}{2\,\Gamma(v/2)}$$
on the half-plane $\mathcal V=\{\operatorname{Re}v>1\}$. It satisfies (K1) and (K2), and (K3) is stated on the strip $2<\operatorname{Re}v<3$ (Proposition 9.1). Its decay up the contour is $e^{-\pi|\operatorname{Im}z|/2}$, the rate of $S$ and half that of $G$, because each Gamma factor sees half the imaginary part. Sections 5 to 8 use nothing about a kernel beyond (K1)–(K3), so sheets, the local model, rotation and the obstruction apply as they stand, and with no constant $C$: the paths in $\{\operatorname{Re}v>2B_\infty\}\setminus\Pi$ stay in the kernel's half-plane. What changes is the arithmetic sum. Now $X_x'(x)=\tfrac12\Theta_2(x)$ with
$$\Theta_2(x)=\sum_{a+b=x}c(a)c(b)\,\mathrm B\big(\tfrac a2,\tfrac b2\big),$$
whose main term at $x=1+u$ with $u\ne1$ is $-2c(u)\,\mathrm B(\tfrac12,\tfrac u2)$ (Lemma 9.2). The far pairs are bounded by $2e^{-H}$ (Lemma 9.3), and the counting argument of Topic 16 gives dense points with $\Theta_2\ne0$ (Theorem 9.4). These points are found separately from those of Theorem 3.1; the paper says they need not be the same points.

*One logarithm, $G_{\log}$.* The weight $\log q$ turns one copy of $P$ into $Q$:
$$G_{\log}(s)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}K_s(z)\,P(z)\,Q(s-z)\,dz\qquad(\operatorname{Re}s>2,\ 1<c<\operatorname{Re}s-1)$$
(Lemma 9.5). The left factor still has logarithms; the right factor has simple poles. The same cuts and separating graphs serve, with every right point kept east of the contour, and the sheet is called $D^\kappa_{\log}$ (Theorem 9.6). At a pinch a logarithm now meets a pole, and the local fact is Lemma 6.2:
$$\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{A_\epsilon}f(t,\epsilon)\big(c_1\operatorname{Log}t+E_1(t)\big)\Big(\frac{c_2}{\epsilon-t}+E_2(\epsilon-t)\Big)dt=c_1c_2\,f(\epsilon,\epsilon)\operatorname{Log}\epsilon+H(\epsilon),$$
with $H$ holomorphic. The pole picks out the *value* of the rest of the integrand at the pole, logarithm included. So the coefficient of the logarithm is a value of the kernel, where for $G$ it was an integral of the kernel:
$$D^\kappa_{\log}(s)=\operatorname{Log}_\kappa(s-x)\,Y_x(s)+H(s),\qquad Y_x(s)=-\sum_{a+b=x}c(a)c(b)\,K_s(s-b)$$
(Theorem 9.7). $Y_x$ is holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, every branch reached along the paths of Theorem 9.8 has the same $Y_x$ in its local description, and $Y_x(x)=-\Theta_G(x)$ (Lemma 9.9), which is minus the sum Topic 16 already controls. The coefficient itself is non-zero at the dense points, where for $G$ only its derivative was. So at such a point, apart from holomorphic terms, $G_{\log}$ behaves like a multiple of $\log(s-x)$, where $G$ behaves like a multiple of $(v-x)\log(v-x)$, and the obstruction needs one step less (Lemma 9.10).

The sign check in Section 9.2 starts from $A(X)=\sum_{p+q\le X}\log q=\sum_{p\le X}\vartheta(X-p)$, where $\vartheta(y)=\sum_{q\le y}\log q$. The prime number theorem gives $\vartheta(y)=y+o(y)$. For any chosen relative error, choose a fixed cutoff above which this error bound holds. Below that cutoff the total error is $O(\pi(X))$, where $\pi(t)$ counts primes up to $t$. Thus $A(X)$ is asymptotic to $\sum_{p\le X}(X-p)=\int_2^X\pi(t)\,dt\sim X^2/(2\log X)$. Partial summation then writes $G_{\log}(2+\delta)$ as $(2+\delta)\int_4^\infty A(t)t^{-3-\delta}\,dt$. The substitution $t=e^y$ leads to $\int_{\log4}^\infty e^{-\delta y}\,dy/y$, which grows like $-\log\delta$ as $\delta\to0^+$. This agrees with $Y_2(2)=-1$ in the local model when $B_\infty<1$. The paper uses the calculation to check the sign and normalization.

The paper compares the three types of collision. For $T$ two poles meet and the obstruction is a residue, $\Theta_G(x)$. For $G_{\log}$ a logarithm meets a pole and the obstruction is the value $Y_x(x)=-\Theta_G(x)$. For $G$, $S$ and $G_2$ two logarithms meet and the obstruction is the derivative $X_x'(x)$ in $v$, which is $\Theta_G(x)$ for $G$, $\tfrac12\Theta_S(x)$ for $S$, and $\tfrac12\Theta_2(x)$ for $G_2$.

**Worked example.** *A pole picks out a value.* By the residue theorem, for a small circle $\gamma$ about $t=\epsilon$ that stays off the cut of the logarithm, and $g$ holomorphic,
$$\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma\frac{g(t)\operatorname{Log}t}{t-\epsilon}\,dt=g(\epsilon)\operatorname{Log}\epsilon.$$
The answer contains $\operatorname{Log}\epsilon$, so as a function of $\epsilon$ it is many-valued around $\epsilon=0$, and the coefficient of the logarithm is the value $g(\epsilon)$. Lemma 6.2 is this fact for the arc $A_\epsilon$ of Topic 14: comparing $A_\epsilon$ with a fixed arc further east makes exactly such a loop about $t=\epsilon$.

*The three sums at the simplest point.* When $B_\infty<1$, the local construction includes $x=2$. The only pair there is $(1,1)$, because every other point of $\Sigma$ has real part below $1$, and $c(1)^2=1$ (this is the point the paper uses for its sign check in Section 6). So
$$\Theta_G(2)=\mathrm B(1,1)=1,\qquad Y_2(2)=-\Theta_G(2)=-1,\qquad\Theta_2(2)=\mathrm B\big(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\big)=\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac12)^2}{\Gamma(1)}=\pi.$$
Reading these with the local models: near $s=2$, $G$ has the singular term $\delta\log\delta$ (Topic 14) and $G_{\log}$ has $-\log\delta$, with $\delta=s-2$; and $G_2$, in its variable $v=2s$ near $v=2$, has $\tfrac\pi2\,\delta\log\delta$ with $\delta=v-2$, since $X_2'(2)=\tfrac12\Theta_2(2)$.

**Where the paper uses it.** Lemma 6.2 (the pole–logarithm pinch) is at the end of Section 6. Section 9.1 is the squares: Proposition 9.1, Lemmas 9.2 and 9.3, Theorem 9.4, and the proof of Theorem 1.3(a),(b) for $G_2$. Section 9.2 is the one-logarithm series: Lemma 9.5, Theorems 9.6 to 9.8, Lemmas 9.9 and 9.10, and the proof for $G_{\log}$. Section 9.3 proves Theorem 1.3(c) for both, by showing that the "good" times along the path, those at which the continued function is admissible at the points near the current one, form a set that is open and closed in $[0,1]$ and contains $0$, hence all of $[0,1]$. The overview is the paragraph "Two more kernels" and Table 1 in Section 1.4.

Section 1.5 refutes El-Gammal's Conjecture 7.8 without any hypothesis on the zeros. The prime number theorem gives a count of order $X/\log^2 X$ for the ordered pairs with $p^2+q^2\le X$. Partial summation gives convergence at $s=1$ and divergence at every real $s<1$. The coefficients are nonnegative, so Landau's theorem makes $s=1$ singular. The finite limit $G_2(1)$ along the real axis from the right excludes a pole. This rules out a single meromorphic continuation to $\Re s>0$. The point $s=1$ belongs to $\mathcal C_2$ because $2=1+1\in\Pi$. Paths can cross $\Re s=1$ at points off the catalogue as in Theorem 1.3(a), and the every-branch boundary is $\Re s=\tfrac12$. The argument at $s=1$ is outside the Lean release.

The point $s=1$ gives a concrete case for reading the path rules. It is a catalogue point and is singular on approach from the convergent series. The continuation statement concerns paths in $\Re s>\tfrac12$ that avoid the catalogue. The final remark concerns any continuation that actually reaches a holomorphic germ, and tests possible extensions from that germ to a disc on the boundary line.

## 19. A map of the proof in one page

The paper does two jobs and joins them at $X_x$, then repeats each job once for a new series.

1. **Objects (Section 2).** Build $P$, $Q=-P'$, the candidate set $\Sigma$, the coefficient $c(a)$ (Lemma 2.1), the growth bound (Lemma 2.2), the two kernels and the Mellin–Barnes formula (K1)–(K3), the pair integrals $J_{ab}$ and the coefficient $X_x$ with $X_x'(x)=\sum_{a+b=x}c(a)c(b)\,\mathcal K(a,x)$, which is $\sum c(a)c(b)\mathrm B(a,b)$ for $G$ and $\tfrac12\sum c(a)c(b)K\big(\tfrac{a-b+3}4\big)$ for $S$ (Section 2.4).
2. **Arithmetic (Section 3).** At a dense set of points $x=1+\rho/q$ near the line, $X_x'(x)\ne0$ (Theorem 3.1), via single representations (Theorem 3.3), near pairs discarded, far pairs negligible (Lemma 3.4).
3. **$T$, the single-valued case (Section 4).** Shift the contour: $T$ continues meromorphically with residue $\sum c(a)c(b)\mathrm B(a,b)$ at each candidate point (Theorems 4.1, 4.2). Dense nonzero residues give poles accumulating at the line: Theorem 1.1.
4. **Sheets (Section 5).** For $G,S$, cut the plane and define the single-valued sheet $D^\kappa$ (Theorem 5.3).
5. **Local model (Section 6).** Near a candidate point, $D^\kappa=\operatorname{Log}_\kappa(v-x)X_x+$ holomorphic (Theorem 6.1), by the pinch calculation. Lemma 6.2 records the pole–logarithm version for later use.
6. **Every branch (Section 7).** Rotating the cut changes the sheet by holomorphic functions $M_x$, so $X_x$ is the same on every branch (Theorems 7.3, 7.4).
7. **Boundary (Section 8).** $X_x'(x)\ne0\Rightarrow X_x\not\equiv0\Rightarrow$ non-trivial monodromy, which no single-valued meromorphic extension can match (Lemma 8.1). A short paragraph before the proof shows that admissible germs of $S$ exist at every point of $\operatorname{Re}s>0$ off the catalogue, so the statement for $S$ is not vacuous. Steering an admissible germ to such a point proves Theorem 1.2, and removing the path restriction gives Theorem 8.2 and Corollary 8.3.
8. **Two more kernels (Section 9).** For $G_2$: a new kernel with the same three properties (Proposition 9.1), a new sum $\Theta_2$ with its main term and far-pair bound (Lemmas 9.2, 9.3), and dense points where it is non-zero (Theorem 9.4); Sections 5 to 8 are reused. For $G_{\log}$: the integral with one factor $Q$ (Lemma 9.5), sheets (Theorem 9.6), the local model with coefficient $Y_x$ from Lemma 6.2 (Theorem 9.7), every branch (Theorem 9.8), $Y_x(x)=-\Theta_G(x)$ (Lemma 9.9) and the obstruction (Lemma 9.10); Section 3 is reused. Together these prove Theorem 1.3(a),(b), and Section 9.3 proves part (c).
9. **Under RH (Section 10).** Every candidate point of $G$ is a genuine branch point, where $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ gave only a dense family (Theorem 10.1); the non-cancellation machinery is what $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ costs over RH.
10. **Lean (Section 11, Appendix A).** Lean checks the implications, including the existence of admissible germs of $S$ and the catalogue-endpoint results for $G_2$ and $G_{\log}$. The written deduction in Corollary 1.4 combines these implications with the separate proof of RH. Topic 18 walks through the join.

## 21. Questions a referee might ask, and where the answers are

- **"Why is $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ the right hypothesis, and how does it relate to RH?"** It is $B_\infty=\tfrac12$ (Sections 1.2 and 2.1). RH and the condition that only finitely many zeros lie off the line each imply it, so it allows exceptions to RH. The paper uses Alpöge’s certified Lean proof of RH to supply the hypothesis. Backlund’s criterion gives Lindelöf from the hypothesis (Section 1.2). The arithmetic needs only $B<1$. The continuation proofs use $B_\infty=\tfrac12$ to reach the line, and the paper says it does not show that $B<1$ alone gets there.
- **"Where exactly does the proof fail under RH's absence to need all this machinery?"** Without RH, several pairs sum to one candidate point and might cancel; Section 1.4 lists the three failure modes, and Section 3 defeats each. Section 10 shows what collapses to one term under RH.
- **"Is the candidate set $\Sigma$ really the singular set, or just candidates?"** The points of $\Sigma$ are candidates. Lemma 2.1(i) says that $Q$ has a pole at $a\in\Sigma$ exactly when $c(a)\ne0$. Section 3 proves non-cancellation of the weighted sums over pairs at selected points $x=1+u$.
- **"On a multivalued function, what does 'natural boundary' even mean?"** No admissible germ extends meromorphically to a disc centred on the boundary line that contains its base point (Theorems 1.2 and 1.3(b)). Theorem 7.4(c) gives a common local coefficient $X_x$ for germs of the sheet family for $G$ and $S$, and Section 9.1 applies that calculation to $G_2$. Theorem 9.8(b)–(c) gives the common coefficient $Y_x$ near a candidate point for $G_{\log}$ on the family reached along catalogue-avoiding paths in the continuation half-plane. Under $(\mathrm H_\infty)$, Corollary 8.3 gives admissibility for arbitrary arrival paths of $G$ and $S$ ending off the catalogue, and Theorem 8.2(a) gives the boundary obstruction for arbitrary endpoints, including catalogue points. For $G_2$ and $G_{\log}$, Theorem 1.3(c) gives admissibility off the catalogue and the closing remark in Section 9.3 gives the obstruction when the endpoint is in the catalogue.
- **"How do you know the coefficient is the same on every branch?"** Two sheets differ by a sum of functions $M_x=2\pi iX_x$ (Theorem 7.3), and every germ reached along the catalogue-avoiding paths of Theorem 7.4 is a sheet plus an integer combination of the $M_x$, plus a constant in the case of $S$. Under $(\mathrm H_\infty)$, Corollary 8.3 gives the same description for arbitrary arrival paths ending off the catalogue in the sheet domain. The $M_x$ are holomorphic throughout $\mathcal V$, and so is a constant, so neither changes a coefficient.
- **"Why the distinctness of the Hardy–Littlewood zeros?"** Section 3 builds distinct points $\rho/q$, and a zero counted $m$ times supplies one point, not $m$. Theorem 3.3(a) gives bounds on the number of pairs. Its proof uses the lower bound for the number of distinct critical-line zeros in (Z4), where the paper also discusses the wording of the cited theorems.
- **"Why include $S$?"** As a stress test of the method on an asymmetric kernel with half the decay, whose sheets the paper builds only on the strip $1<\operatorname{Re}v<3$, where (K1) holds on the whole range $0<\operatorname{Re}z<\operatorname{Re}v$. The constant $C$ for $S$ in Section 7 is where that bites: a path can leave the strip and come back with a different constant.
- **"Why two more series, $G_2$ and $G_{\log}$?"** Each changes one layer of the argument and leaves the others alone, which shows what depends on what (the opening of Section 9, and Table 1). $G_2$ changes the kernel, so only the arithmetic is redone. $G_{\log}$ changes the local calculation, a logarithm meeting a pole, and reuses the arithmetic of $G$ and $T$.
- **"Is there one family of points that works for all the sums?"** Theorem 3.1 gives points where $\Theta_G$ and $\Theta_S$ are both non-zero. For $\Theta_2$ the paper finds its points separately and says they need not be the same (after Theorem 9.4). Each boundary theorem needs only its own sum.
- **"Does the family $D^\kappa+m+C$ classify the branches?"** No, and the paper says so before Theorem 7.4: the family contains every germ reached along a path, but the paper does not claim that every $m$ and every $C$ is reached.
- **"How does this sit with what is in print about the squares case?"** Section 1.5: Tanigawa and Zhai state, for the von Mangoldt-weighted analogue of $G_2$ under RH, continuation only to $\operatorname{Re}s>\tfrac12$, and Egami and Matsumoto wrote that the proof of the natural-boundary claim of Tanigawa and Zhai's Theorem 1.3 "seems incomplete". El-Gammal's conjecture of a single meromorphic continuation to $\operatorname{Re}s>0$ fails at $s=1$ by the argument in Section 1.5. Topic 17 explains how continuation along paths can still pass the line $\operatorname{Re}s=1$ away from the catalogue.
- **"What is machine-checked, and what is not?"** Section 11 lists the implications from $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ and the applicable inputs among (Z1)–(Z6), and Theorem 10.1 from RH alone. The catalogue-endpoint declarations for $G_2$ and $G_{\log}$ are also checked. Corollary 1.4 joins the two Lean builds by the written steps in Topic 18. The axiom check lists the three standard axioms. Whether the Lean statements say what the prose says is a matter of reading them (Appendix A). The statement on the use of AI (the unnumbered section after Section 11) says two separate things: the author did not write or check the Lean development line by line, and relies on the Lean kernel and the checks described there; and no human mathematician has checked the proof line by line.
- **"What does the proof say about a catalogue endpoint for the two further series?"** The good time argument gives a disc of the representative at the endpoint, with admissible germs off the catalogue. A meromorphic extension of the endpoint germ would extend one of those neighbours in the same boundary-centred disc and contradict Theorem 1.3(b). See the remark after the proof of part (c) in Section 9.3 and its Lean declarations in Section 11.
- **"Who did the work?"** The statement on the use of AI (after Section 11): the author directed the research, chose the direction and designed the verification; AI systems found, reviewed, drafted and formalized; no AI system is an author; the author takes full responsibility.

Answers follow each question; cover them and try first.

**Q1.** Why can the series $G$, inside the half-plane where it is continued, be singular only at points of the form $a+b$ with $a,b\in\Sigma$?

**Q2.** Give the value of $c(1)$ and explain its sign.

**Q3.** State $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ in terms of $B_\infty$, say why RH implies it, and describe how the finiteness condition allows a hypothetical configuration with off-line zeros.

**Q4.** In one sentence, what is the difference between the singularity of $Q$ at a point $a\in\Sigma$ with $c(a)\ne0$ and the singularity of $P$ there?

**Q5.** Why must the cut direction $\kappa$ be a genuine slope, not horizontal?

**Q6.** How does the paper read off the coefficient $X_x$ from the branch, and why does a single-valued meromorphic extension across the line force $X_x\equiv0$?

**Q7.** Why does the paper count the Hardy–Littlewood zeros of (Z4) *distinctly*?

**Q8.** In the three-step non-cancellation argument, what plays the role of the "additive relations among ordinates" hypothesis of earlier work?

**Q9.** For $G_2$, which parts of the proof are reused unchanged and which are redone? Why is there no constant $C$?

**Q10.** For $G_{\log}$, why is the coefficient of the logarithm a value of the kernel, and why does the series need no new arithmetic?

**Q11.** What are the three steps outside Lean in the deduction of Corollary 1.4?

---

**A1.** In the Mellin–Barnes integrand $\mathcal K(z,v)P(z)P(v-z)$, the first copy of $P$ has candidate singularities at the left points $z=a\in\Sigma$ and the second copy at the right points $z=v-b$ with $b\in\Sigma$. The contour can be pinched only when a right point meets a left point, i.e. $a=v-b$, which is $v=a+b$. This concerns points inside the half-plane of continuation, where these sums are the candidates; the boundary line is a separate matter, settled by Theorem 1.2. Topic 8 and Section 5.2 describe the cut geometry. Section 6 and the proof of Theorem 6.1 give the collision criterion and the local pinch calculation. Theorem 7.4 describes the reached germs and their common local coefficient.

**A2.** $c(1)=-1$. The only representation of $1$ is $k=1$, and $\operatorname{ord}_1\zeta=-1$ because $\zeta$ has a pole (not a zero) at $1$; so $c(1)=\mu(1)\cdot(-1)=-1$, giving $Q$ residue $R_1=+1$. (Topic 3; Lemma 2.1.)

**A3.** $(\mathrm{H}_\infty)$ is $B_\infty=\tfrac12$: the supremum of the real parts of the zeros of height at least $t$ tends to $\tfrac12$ as $t\to\infty$. RH makes every non-trivial zero have real part $\tfrac12$, so $B_\infty=\tfrac12$. Finitely many zeros off the line, with all the others on it, would give $B_\infty=\tfrac12$ and some $B<1$ above $\tfrac12$: a hypothetical configuration satisfying the finiteness condition and violating RH. That is the sense in which the hypothesis allows exceptions to RH. This describes the scope of the finiteness condition used in the boundary proofs. In Corollary 1.4, Alpöge’s certified Lean proof supplies RH, which implies this condition directly. (Topic 4.)

**A4.** At $a\in\Sigma$ with $c(a)\ne0$, $Q$ has a simple *pole* (a meromorphic, single-valued singularity) and $P$ has a *logarithmic branch point* (a multivalued singularity). Since $Q=-P'$, differentiation turns the one into the other. ($G$ is different: its branch points are at the sums $x\in\Pi=\Sigma+\Sigma$ with nonzero coefficient, not at points of $\Sigma$.) (Topics 2, 3.)

**A5.** With a horizontal cut, the points $1/k$ all lie on the real axis, and an off-line zero $\rho$ shares its imaginary part with its mirror $1-\bar\rho$, so distinct singular points would fall on one cut-line and genericity would fail; a slope separates them. (Topic 13; Section 5.1.)

**A6.** Across the slit the holomorphic part $H$ is continuous and $\operatorname{Log}_\kappa$ jumps by $2\pi i$, so the branch jumps by $2\pi i\,X_x$; the coefficient is that jump over $2\pi i$. A function meromorphic on a disc straddling the line is single-valued, so it has no jump; agreeing with the branch would force $X_x=0$ on a segment, hence $X_x\equiv0$ near $x$ by the identity theorem. (Topics 11, 15; Lemma 8.1.)

**A7.** Section 3 needs many *different* points $x=1+\rho/q$, built from different zeros $\rho$; a single high-multiplicity zero counted many times would give one point, not many. The count $N_0(t)\ge A_0t$ of distinct zeros is what the paper's bound $\gg X^2/\log^2X$ on the number of pairs $(\rho,q)$ uses, and that many pairs outvote the rare spoilers. (Topic 5, (Z4); Theorem 3.3.)

**A8.** The count of near pairs. The proof gets many distinct points $\rho/q$ from (Z4) and (Z5). It counts at most $\log^{O(1)}X$ of them with a competing low-height pair and discards those few. Earlier work used a hypothesis on additive relations among ordinates to exclude near pairs. Section 3.3 gives the count and compares it with the earlier hypothesis, whose historical context is in Section 1.5. See also Topic 16.

**A9.** Reused: sheets, the local model, rotation, every branch and the obstruction (Sections 5 to 8), because they use only the kernel facts (K1)–(K3), which Proposition 9.1 supplies; and, inside the arithmetic, the count of good pairs and of near pairs, which depend on the points and not on the kernel. Redone: the main term, now $-2c(u)\mathrm B(\tfrac12,\tfrac u2)$, and the far-pair bound (Lemmas 9.2 and 9.3). There is no constant $C$ because the kernel's domain is the half-plane $\operatorname{Re}v>1$, so a path in $\{\operatorname{Re}v>2B_\infty\}\setminus\Pi$ stays in the region where the sheets are defined; for $S$ the domain was a strip. (Topic 17; Section 9.1.)

**A10.** One factor of the integrand is $Q$, with a simple pole where $P$ has a logarithm. When the pole of the right factor meets the logarithm of the left factor, the residue theorem evaluates the rest of the integrand at the pole (Lemma 6.2), giving $\operatorname{Log}$ times the kernel value $K_s(s-b)$. At $s=x$ that value is $\mathrm B(a,b)$, so $Y_x(x)=-\Theta_G(x)$, and Theorem 3.1 already gives dense points where $\Theta_G\ne0$. (Topic 17; Section 9.2, Lemma 9.9.)

**A11.** Section 11 lists the comparison of the source definition of `RiemannHypothesis` in the two Mathlib versions, the deduction of $(\mathrm H_\infty)$ from RH, and the use of the applicable classical inputs (Z1)–(Z6). The first compares text across the two separate builds. The second uses the fact that RH makes the set of zeros above each positive margin empty. The third supplies the hypotheses of the boundary implications from their cited sources.
